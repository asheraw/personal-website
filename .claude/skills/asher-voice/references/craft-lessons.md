# Craft lessons (Zinsser + Provost)

Distilled from William Zinsser, *On Writing Well* (30th anniversary edition), and Gary Provost, *100 Ways to Improve Your Writing* (updated edition). Paraphrased and selected for what actually helps Asher's writing. Where the books clash with his voice, his voice wins; see the table at the end.

## 1. Before drafting

- **Know the one point.** Zinsser's core question: what am I trying to say? Clear thinking comes before clear writing. If the point can't be stated in one sentence, the draft will wander.
- **Picture one reader.** Provost advises writing to a specific person. This lines up with Asher's 1-to-1 rule.
- **Think narrow.** Zinsser on memoir: it's a window into a life, not a summary of it. Pick a corner of the experience and go deep. This is also how short-form works without compressing.
- **Collect more than you'll use.** Zinsser: a piece is strong in proportion to the surplus of detail you can choose from. Ask Asher for more detail than you think you need, then choose.
- **Ask why you're writing.** Provost: to entertain, inform, persuade? Knowing the purpose tells you what to cut.

## 2. The lead

- **The first sentence has one job:** make the reader read the second. Then the second pulls into the third (Zinsser).
- **Give them something to care about before background** (Provost): either something that affects them, or a person they can identify with. Background comes later, if at all.
- **The lead must do real work** (Zinsser): after the hook, give the hard detail that tells the reader why this piece exists.
- **Don't promise what you can't deliver** (Provost). A hook bigger than the body is a trick, and readers feel cheated.
- **Start inside the action.** Zinsser warns against the "breakfast-to-bed" opening that begins with the alarm clock. Just tell the story, starting where it gets interesting.
- **Retire tired leads.** Zinsser lists ones he never wants to see again: the future archaeologist, the visitor from Mars, the cute thing that happened "one day not long ago", the "what do these famous people have in common?" setup. Add the modern AI equivalents: "In today's fast-paced world", "Let's be honest", "Picture this".

## 3. The middle

- **Every paragraph builds on the last** (Zinsser). If one doesn't move the story or the point forward, cut it.
- **The last sentence of each paragraph is a springboard.** Zinsser suggests giving it a small twist of humour or surprise so the reader carries on.
- **Show, don't tell** (Provost). Put the reader in the scene rather than reporting what it meant.
- **Write about people** (Provost). Readers care about people more than ideas; an idea lands through a person doing something.
- **Use anecdotes and examples** (Provost). A general claim without an example is easy to ignore.
- **Sensory detail** (Zinsser, memoir chapter). A sound or a smell can carry a whole memory, as long as it shaped the moment.
- **Every detail does useful work** (Zinsser). Zinsser's line between ego and egotism: confessional writing is healthy when every detail moves the story; it becomes self-indulgent when it's there only to be said. This is how Asher's long, detailed posts stay readable.
- **Show your opinion** (Provost). Writing without a view is dull, and hiding it is often dishonest. Asher's takes should be stated plainly, with room for people who disagree.
- **Transitions shouldn't hide gaps** (Provost). If moving from one section to the next feels forced, something is missing. Add the missing explanation instead of papering over it with "Anyway..."

## 4. Tone and voice

- **Same voice on every subject** (Zinsser). Don't write AI posts in "tech voice" and faith posts in "church voice". It's the same Asher.
- **Unity of pronoun, tense and mood** (Zinsser). Choose them and stick with them. Glances backward and forward in time are fine; switching the main tense is not. Don't let a personal story suddenly turn into a guidebook or lecture.
- **Set a tone and keep it** (Provost). Mood can change, but signal the change (Zinsser's "mood changers": but, yet, still, now, later).
- **Casual is earned, not faked** (Zinsser on the "breezy" style). The relaxed voice that sounds effortless takes discipline. Slang piled on to sound friendly reads as corny and condescending. Zinsser stops reading writers who say "you see".
- **Don't force a style** (Provost). Style comes from writing well and honestly, not from imitating someone. This is why the skill protects Asher's real habits instead of adding flourishes.
- **Humour sneaks up** (Zinsser). Understatement lands; announced jokes don't.

## 5. Sentences and words

- **Short, familiar words** (both). "Stop" beats "discontinue". Plain words are stronger.
- **Strong, active verbs** (both). Verbs push the sentence forward. Active voice most of the time.
- **Specific nouns** (Provost). "A secondhand ThinkPad" beats "a laptop".
- **Concept nouns kill sentences** (Zinsser). Sentences built on "reaction", "response", "hostility" have no people and no action in them. Rewrite so someone does something.
- **Cut redundant adverbs and adjectives** (Zinsser). "Blared loudly", "clenched tightly" - the verb already says it.
- **Prune little qualifiers** (Zinsser): a bit, sort of, kind of, rather, quite, very, pretty much. They dilute feeling. Be tired, not a bit tired. (See conflicts table for Asher's deliberate "kinda".)
- **Emphatic words go at the end** (Provost). Readers hear the last word of a sentence loudest.
- **Vary sentence length** (Provost). Uniform sentences drone. Mix short and long, and let a long one build to a short one that hits.
- **Keep related words together** (Provost), and use parallel construction for lists and rule-of-three.
- **Say things positively, most of the time** (Provost). "He forgot" beats "he did not remember".
- **Contractions warm the voice** (Zinsser). "I'll", "can't", "didn't".
- **Don't intrude** (Provost). Typos, obscure words, and the same word used two ways in a row all break the reader's trance.
- **The quickest fix** (Zinsser). If a sentence won't behave, ask whether it's needed at all. Often it isn't.

## 6. The ending

- **When you're ready to stop, stop** (Zinsser). A piece that keeps concluding after it has concluded becomes a drag.
- **No recap** (Zinsser). "In sum" signals you're about to repeat what the reader just read, and they leave.
- **The cut-back test** (Provost). Ask what the reader loses if the last sentence goes. If nothing, cut it and test the next one up. When you reach one that must stay, check it sounds final and leaves the mood you want.
- **Full circle** (Zinsser). Echo a note from the beginning. This is Asher's callback ending.
- **Surprising yet right** (Zinsser). A good ending feels slightly unexpected and exactly correct.

## 7. Revision

- **Rewriting is the job** (Zinsser). First drafts are almost never right. Expect to rewrite.
- **Read it out loud** (both). The ear catches clumsy rhythm, repeated words and sentences nobody would say.
- **Provost's pre-final questions**, adapted:
  1. Is it clear early what this is about?
  2. Does each paragraph move it forward?
  3. Do the important moments stand out?
  4. Does it need more detail, example or story anywhere?
  5. Are there sweeping claims that need support?
  6. Is any repetition unintended?
  7. Is the tone consistent?
  8. Is any sentence hard to follow on first read?
  9. Are any words vague?

## 8. Honesty

- **Don't cheat** (Provost). Don't mislead the reader or hide facts.
- **Don't overstate** (Zinsser). Inflating an incident to make it funnier or more dramatic costs credibility, and one bogus claim makes everything else suspect.
- **Don't play the mystery game** (Provost). Withholding the point to keep readers hooked mostly distracts them.
- **Name your sources** (Provost). No "studies show" without the study.

## 9. Where the books and Asher disagree - and who wins

| Craft advice | Asher's voice | Resolution |
|---|---|---|
| Zinsser: clutter is the disease; cut, cut, cut | Posts run 800-1,500 words; don't compress | **Both.** Cut clutter *inside* sentences; keep the scenes. Length comes from detail and story, never from wordiness. A long post with no clutter is the goal. |
| Zinsser: prune little qualifiers ("kind of", "a bit") | "kinda like", "weeee bit" are his colour | Keep his deliberate colour words, sprinkled. Cut qualifiers that hedge a feeling or claim ("a bit anxious", "sort of important"). |
| Zinsser: likes the dash | No em dashes, ever | **Asher wins.** Hyphen with spaces, comma, colon, or split the sentence. |
| Provost: avoid parentheses | Light joke asides in brackets | Allow 1-2 per post, jokes or real-time asides only. Otherwise fold the aside into the sentence. |
| Provost: the "Tom Wolfe game" (stretched spelling, caps) usually fails | "aaaaannd.... blank." at the turning point | Allowed once, at the pivotal beat, for timing. Provost's own test is "can you state a good reason?" - comic or suspense timing is the reason. |
| Provost: write complete sentences | Fragments for punch | Fragments as accents after a build-up. Not as the default unit. |
| Provost: don't play the mystery game | Open-loop transitions | Open loops resolve in the next section. The post's main point is never withheld until the end. |
| Zinsser: beware the breezy style | "haha", "lemme", "Alrighty" | His tics are real speech, sprinkled. The AI failure is piling on slang to sound friendly. If it sounds performed, cut it. |
| Zinsser: don't overstate | Dramatised pivotal scenes ("a thousand emotions running rampant") | Intensity is fine when it matches what Asher felt and told you. Never add hyperbole he didn't give you. |
| Provost: use short paragraphs | Short paragraphs as emphasis only | Short paragraphs, yes; one-line paragraphs, only at emphasis points. |
| Generic slop rules: cut "honestly", "actually" | Honesty markers carry real weight for him | Keep when they signal a real admission or emphasis. |
