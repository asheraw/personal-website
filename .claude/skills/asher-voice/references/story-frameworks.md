# Story and copywriting frameworks

Frameworks are skeletons. The reader should never see the bones. If a draft reads like someone ticking boxes ("Problem. Agitate. Solve."), the framework is showing - loosen it.

Two families live here:

- **Story frameworks** (3-Act, Story Spine, PAS, Hero's Journey) - for blog posts, newsletters, and any piece built on something that happened to Asher.
- **Copywriting frameworks** (BAB, Open Loop, AIDA, StoryBrand, ACCA, 4Ps, FAB) - for platform posts, teaching content, offers and about pages.

A lot of Asher's platform content is both: a short story carrying a teaching point. In that case pick the story framework for the shape and borrow one move from a copywriting framework (usually the hook or the call to action).

## Quick selection

| Goal of the piece | First choice | Also works |
|---|---|---|
| Tell something that happened, general | 3-Act | Story Spine |
| A chain of events where one thing led to another | Story Spine | 3-Act |
| A practical frustration and a change (tool, habit, workflow, decision) | PAS | BAB |
| A major life turning point, long reflective post | Hero's Journey | 3-Act |
| Transformation, case study, client result | BAB | PAS |
| Curiosity, series posts, hook-heavy platform content | Open Loop | PAS |
| Teach a concept to people who don't know they need it yet | AIDA | ACCA |
| Build trust, thought leadership, change a belief | ACCA | PAS |
| About page, brand story, offer page | StoryBrand | Hero's Journey |
| Launch or sales post | 4Ps | BAB |
| Explain a service or feature | FAB | 4Ps |

Stakes check: if a piece is soft-selling, a story framework usually beats a sales framework. Asher's audience trusts him because he educates first.

## Story frameworks

### 3-Act structure (default)

**Shape:** Ordinary world → rising complication and struggle → resolution (or honest non-resolution).

**When:** The default for most confessional or personal-development posts. Confirmed by feedback as the shape Asher wants: it wraps the beginning and the end, and following it makes the post easy to follow.

**Asher adaptation:**
- Act 1 is short. Hook, then just enough "ordinary world" for the reader to feel the stakes.
- Act 2 is the longest. This is where the felt experience, sensory detail and the pivotal scene live. Don't compress it.
- Act 3 lands the earned realisation and closes on a callback to Act 1.

**Pitfall:** A thin Act 2. If Act 2 is shorter than Act 1, the draft is a highlight reel.

### Story Spine

**Shape:** Once upon a time... Every day... Until one day... Because of that... Because of that... Until finally... And ever since then...

**When:** A chronological account with a clear inciting incident and cascading consequences. Example from Asher's blog: "How I Lost My Writing Home".

**Asher adaptation:** The "because of that" links are the story. Give each link its own beat with a concrete detail, rather than listing the consequences in one paragraph.

**Pitfall:** Turning the chain into a timeline ("then... then... then..."). Each step must be caused by the one before it.

### PAS (Problem → Agitate → Solve)

**Shape:** Name the problem → make the reader feel what it costs → show the change.

**When:** A practical frustration and a change: a tool or workflow post, a decision, a habit. Pain-led nurture posts. Engagement posts where the agitation is the talking point.

**Asher adaptation:**
- The problem is usually his own first ("Confession: I..."), then turned toward the reader with "you".
- Agitate with his real experience, not with scare tactics aimed at the reader. The cost should be specific: the hours, the money, the embarrassing moment.
- "Solve" can be partial. An honest "here's where I am now" beats a fake clean fix.

**Pitfall:** Skipping Agitate. It is the middle, and it is where the story lives.

### Hero's Journey

**Shape:** Ordinary world → call → refusal or struggle → trials → transformation → return with something to share.

**When:** Bigger reflective posts about a major turning point, founder story, "why I do what I do". Heavy: don't force it onto a short piece.

**Asher adaptation:** Asher is the hero of his own past story, but in anything aimed at clients, the reader is the hero and Asher is the guide (see StoryBrand). The "return" is what he now offers the reader, stated plainly.

**Pitfall:** Grandiosity. Keep the trials concrete and human-sized.

## Copywriting frameworks

### BAB (Before → After → Bridge)

**Shape:** Life before → life after → what got you from one to the other.

**When:** Transformation stories, before/after, case studies, soft selling.

**Asher adaptation:** The "Before" must be vivid enough to recognise. The "Bridge" is often the method he teaches - describe it, don't hype it. Client results only if Asher supplies them.

**Pitfall:** A vague "After". Use a concrete change (what they do now that they didn't before), not a feeling of "transformation".

### Open Loop

**Shape:** Raise a question → build tension → resolve.

**When:** Hooks, series posts, curiosity-driven platform content, the transition into a pivotal scene.

**Asher adaptation:** Resolve within the same piece, usually within the next section. For a series, each part must pay off its own loop while opening the next.

**Pitfall:** Provost's "mystery game" - withholding the point of the whole piece so the reader has to wait for it. The reader gets distracted and leaves. Open loops create pull; mystery games create frustration.

### AIDA (Attention → Interest → Desire → Action)

**Shape:** Grab attention → make it relevant → make them want the outcome → one action.

**When:** Teaching posts that end in a clear next step, ads, awareness-to-conversion content.

**Asher adaptation:** "Desire" comes from showing the outcome through a real example, not from promises. The Action is a small, easy step (comment, save, reply), not a hard sell, unless it's a launch.

**Pitfall:** Hype in the Desire stage. His audience distrusts it.

### StoryBrand (Character → Problem → Guide → Plan → Call to action → Success)

**Shape:** The customer is the hero, with a problem. Asher is the guide with empathy and authority. He gives a simple plan and a call to action, and shows what success looks like.

**When:** About pages, bios, offer pages, brand narratives.

**Asher adaptation:** Empathy comes easily because he has been where his reader is (the working professional building a brand on the side). Authority comes from specifics he supplies: 15+ years in marketing, brands he has worked on, learners trained. Never invent credentials.

**Pitfall:** Making Asher the hero of the about page. The reader should see themselves first.

### ACCA (Awareness → Comprehension → Conviction → Action)

**Shape:** Make them aware of an issue → help them understand it → build conviction with reasoning or proof → action.

**When:** Thought leadership, changing a belief, explaining why his approach differs.

**Asher adaptation:** Comprehension is his strength as a trainer - explaining hard ideas simply, with an analogy. Conviction comes from reasoning and real examples, not from "experts agree".

**Pitfall:** Lecturing. Keep it 1-to-1 and anchored in his own experience.

### 4Ps (Problem → Promise → Proof → Proposal)

**Shape:** The problem → what's possible → evidence it works → the offer.

**When:** Launch posts, sales pages, conversion content.

**Asher adaptation:**
- Promise only what the Proof supports (Provost: a lead that can't deliver is a trick).
- Proof must be real: his own results, client words he supplies, or plainly stated reasoning. If there is no proof yet, say what the offer is and who it's for, and skip the fake testimonial.
- No fake scarcity or countdown pressure. If there is a real deadline or a real cap, state it plainly.

**Pitfall:** Overpromising. It costs more trust than it earns sales.

### FAB (Feature → Advantage → Benefit)

**Shape:** What it is → what it does → what that means for you.

**When:** Explaining a service, tool, or programme.

**Asher adaptation:** Always finish on the Benefit in the reader's own words. Pair with a short example so it isn't a spec sheet.

**Pitfall:** Stopping at Features.
