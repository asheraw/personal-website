# Voice examples, by pattern

Pulled directly from asheraw.com/blog. 2026 posts are the current target voice (written by Asher, AI-polished as part of his workflow). Older posts (2013–2019) are included to show a pattern predates AI involvement — if something shows up in both eras, it's Asher, not the tool.

## Hook openers

- "If there's one word to sum up the past few weeks, it's distraction." (2026)
- "I used to write almost every single day. Then... I just stopped." (2026)
- "Have you come across any news that people were becoming delulu after using A.I.?" (2026)
- ">'An actor who teaches. A teacher who acts.' That's the line a free AI tool wrote for my website on the first try." (2026, opens on a blockquote)
- "Initially, I didn't want to write this post." (2016)

## Callback endings (reuse an image already planted, don't invent a new one)

- Setup (2026, "How I Lost My Writing Home"): "It's kinda like… finally feeling confident enough to date again after going through a horrible divorce." Payoff two lines later: "You don't suddenly become perfect. You just stop dreading the experience."
- Setup (2026, "Even My Discipline Was an Escape?"): opens on distraction as the theme. Closes: "I have these written on my wall, not a rule I'm enforcing, but a reminder of what I already know." — lands the discipline/escape tension from the title without restating it.
- Setup (2026, "Angel-Devil Your AI"): opens declaring "This isn't a post about which LLM is better." Closes: "Just like I mentioned at the start… This post is not about which LLM is better. It's that this is ongoing." — literal callback to the opening line.

## Fragments used for punch

- "Oof, hits hard."
- "It was fun while it lasted. But I needed to focus."
- "Not my strength or my preparation, but his provision when I had nothing left." (2018)
- "Another launch. Another promise. Another dramatic situation." (2026, "How I Lost My Writing Home")
- "The platform? Still. Rigged. With. Bugs." (2026 — extreme fragmentation, used exactly once for comic emphasis, not a default rhythm)

## Ellipses as a real pause

- "And it took me … too long… to understand why."
- "Old habits die hard, you know?"
- "I don't know how I'd never positioned it that way before. \nI loved it from the get-go."
- "…which my parents never did, haha, I was a good kid (mostly)."

## Sentences opening with And / But / So / Because

- "But underneath the fun, my real goal kept gnawing at the back of my mind."
- "So I split the work."
- "And it's exactly why I almost never paid for AI at all."
- "Because it was free, I kept hitting notifications telling me to come back later."

## Concrete specifics over vague claims

- "288 days straight" (not "a long time")
- "6 kilograms in roughly a month" / "75.2 kg" to "69.2 kg" (2018)
- "ChatGPT's 1,500-character limit" (not "a short limit")
- "28 July 2026 - Production Log" (dated, not "recently")

## Direct reader address at a landing point

- "You don't suddenly become perfect. You just stop dreading the experience."
- "If you're one of the few people who still reads blogs in 2026 instead of a TikTok or IG video update… thanks."
- "Remember to customise it for your own because this was customised for me."

- "P.S. Oh yeah, one other small thing… If you're one of the few people who still reads blogs in 2026..." (2026, "How I Lost My Writing Home" — a real afterthought, not a running post structure)

## Dramatized pivotal scenes (confirmed by direct feedback — this is the pattern the first skill draft missed)

The richest real example is the ketosis collapse in "Easter 2018: Under the Sycamore Tree" — notice how it slows down instead of summarizing "I felt faint backstage":

> "On the day of the third show, during makeup application, I started to feel faint and giddy. My body was looking for sugar it didn't have. I had to sit down. The makeup team helped me recover — they got me some food with sugar content, got my blood flowing again. But here's what happened in that moment: I was alone, holding the cross during preparation, and I was thinking about Wee Kee... In that vulnerable moment, I prayed."

Same post, the backstage costume-change scramble — real-time, not summarized:

> "Between the crucifixion scene and the final resurrection scene, I had maybe thirty seconds to get cleaned up... On the first show, we didn't make it in time. I was still being cleaned up when I needed to be on stage... Then, as I head up, my cloak got caught on one of the stage lights. By the time I got myself untangled, I made a judgment call: I put the cloak on my arm and proceeded."

Asher's own note on what he wants at a pivotal moment, given as direct feedback on a draft that compressed a scene too fast: *"Dress rehearsal time. I got on stage, got to position, waited for the moment... aaaaannd.... blank. I only had one line. Just one. It was gone. I froze for a second, but a thousand emotions were running rampant in the head trying to find the line and then..."* — note the exaggerated "aaaaannd" and the trailing "and then..." as suspense devices used specifically at the turning point, not as a general tic.

## Open-loop line before a dramatized scene

Confirmed by direct feedback as a pattern he likes in a reviewed draft: "Then came dress rehearsal, and the moment I'll probably remember longest out of this whole run." — states that something big is coming without resolving it, then the next section delivers.

## Never em dashes (confirmed by direct feedback)

Asher does not use the em dash character (—) at all — not because of taste, but because it isn't easily accessible on his keyboard, so he simply never types it. He uses a plain hyphen with spaces instead. None of the excerpts in this file, or in his real posts, contain a literal em dash; treat this as a hard mechanical rule, not a stylistic preference to weigh against others.

## British/Singapore English spelling (confirmed by direct feedback)

Real examples from his own posts: "organised" ("My church's Drama Ministry organised a Silence and Solitude retreat"), "realised" ("I realised this was problematic"), "recognised" ("she probably recognised the crest"), "analyse" ("I got it to analyse my old instructions first"), "customised" ("Created and customised the CMS"). Never the American "-ize"/"-or" forms. He also flagged American-only idioms (e.g. "card on file") as things he wouldn't say even when the spelling is fine.

## Always 1-to-1, never "we"/"us" (confirmed by direct feedback, round 2)

Direct quote: *"it's 1 to 1, not 1 to many... Not 'us', not 'we', but 'me to you' kind of approach."* He praised a reviewed ending specifically because it worked as "a message to potential readers and speaking to that one person." Even on broadly relatable topics, keep the address singular ("you"), never collective ("we all know the feeling," "us AI trainers"). This is easy to slip into unconsciously in a conclusion or call-to-action — check endings specifically.

## Numerals over spelled-out numbers (confirmed by direct feedback, round 2)

Direct quote: *"when I can use numbers, I would. I don't always spell out day one, day two... I use day 1, day 2, day 3... numbers are faster."* Use "10 times," "day 4," "6 kilograms" rather than "ten times," "day four," "six kilograms" — applies generally to counts and sequences, not just this one example.
