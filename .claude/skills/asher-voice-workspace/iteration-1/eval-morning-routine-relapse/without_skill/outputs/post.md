# The Four-Day Pattern

I've counted it now. Roughly ten times this year, I've committed to waking up early to pray and journal before work. Ten times I've done it for about four days straight. Ten times it's fallen apart after that.

It's not that I fail immediately. Day one is easy — day one is always easy. Day two, day three, day four, I'm still going, and I start to believe this is the time it sticks. Then somewhere around day five or six, it just... doesn't happen. Not from some dramatic collapse. I just don't get up. And a week later I'm back to whatever my normal morning was before I decided to change it.

What I finally noticed, after enough repetitions to call it a pattern instead of bad luck, is where these attempts start. They don't start on a random Tuesday because I read a good book or felt vaguely convicted. They start right after something that put me in a spiritually high place — a church retreat, most often. I come back from a weekend like that genuinely lit up. The motivation isn't fake. I mean it completely in the moment. I set the alarm, I tell myself this is different this time, and for four days it actually is different.

Then normal life reasserts itself. Work picks back up, the emails start again, the tiredness that a retreat weekend doesn't actually cure comes back, and the version of me that made the commitment on Sunday night isn't the same version of me trying to execute it on a random Thursday. Within a week, the routine that felt inevitable three days ago feels optional again, and then it's gone.

I don't have this fixed. I want to be upfront about that, because the easy thing to do here would be to wrap this up with three tips and pretend I've cracked it. I haven't. I'm ten repetitions in and the honest answer is I still don't know what makes day five different from day four.

But I have a theory, even if it's unproven. I think what I'm doing in those four days isn't building a habit — it's trying to keep the retreat going. The early wake-up, the prayer, the journaling, all of it is downstream of a feeling I had at a specific moment in a specific place with specific people around me. And feelings like that don't transfer cleanly into a Tuesday morning at home. So for a few days I can run on the fumes of it. Then the fumes run out, because fumes were never going to be the fuel source for something meant to last a year.

If that's true, then the thing I've actually been building ten times isn't a habit at all. It's a monument to how I felt at the retreat. And monuments don't need maintenance the way habits do — they just need to have existed once. Which might be why it's so easy to start and so hard to keep going: starting is remembering the feeling, and continuing is something else entirely, something I haven't figured out how to do without the feeling attached to it.

I don't know yet what building it for the version of me on an ordinary Wednesday actually looks like. I suspect it has something to do with making the thing itself less dependent on how I feel when I wake up, and more just... a thing that happens, regardless. But I'm aware that sentence is easy to write and hard to live, and I've written some version of it in my journal probably eight of the ten times already.

So this isn't a post about how I solved it. It's a post about finally seeing the shape of the thing I keep doing. Ten times is enough to stop calling it a fluke. I'm not sure yet what number eleven needs to look like differently — I just know it can't start the same way the first ten did.
