# The Line I Forgot (And Why the Director Wants Me to Keep Forgetting It)

This year's Christmas drama rehearsals at church started with a small letdown. I auditioned for one of the lead roles, walked in with a version of the character already forming in my head, and then found out I'd been cast as a side character instead. Not the end of the world, obviously, but there's a particular flavor of deflation that comes from picturing yourself in a part and then getting handed a different script.

I won't pretend I wasn't a bit disappointed. I was. For the first rehearsal or two, I think I was still quietly grieving the role I didn't get instead of paying attention to the one I did.

But somewhere in the process of learning my actual lines, something shifted. A side character doesn't come with much backstory built in — the script gives you a name, a few lines, maybe a general sense of where you fit in the story. Everything else is blank space. So I started filling it in. Why is this person here? What do they want? What are they thinking in the scenes where they're not even speaking? None of that ends up explicitly on stage, but it changes how you carry yourself in every scene, and honestly, it turned into the most fun part of the whole process. I ended up enjoying building out this character more than I expected to, maybe more than I would have enjoyed just reciting a lead's lines on autopilot.

Then came dress rehearsal, and the moment I'll probably remember longest out of this whole run.

I had exactly one line. One. And when the moment came, it just wasn't there. Blank. I had a half-second of pure panic standing on stage, mid-scene, with everyone waiting — and instead of freezing completely, I just said something. Not the scripted line. Whatever came out in that instant.

And it worked. Somehow it landed better dramatically than the line that was actually written.

The director cracked up about it afterward, which was its own kind of relief, and then said to just keep it in. My accidental, panicked improvisation is now, apparently, the line.

There's something fitting about that whole arc — going in wanting the part I didn't get, resenting the smaller role for a beat, and then having the smaller role hand me the best moment of the whole rehearsal process, and not even a moment I planned. I didn't build that line. I built the backstory, the character, the intention behind every scene — and then the character built the line for me, right when I needed it most.

Christmas drama season isn't over yet, but if this is the shape it's taking, I'm not mad about how the casting worked out anymore.
